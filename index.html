What actually changed

Three shifts in one release: a new base year, new source data, and stricter quarterly math.

New base: 2022–23

Old anchor 2011–12 retired. All levels and weights now reflect the post-GST, post-Covid economy.

Fresh inputs

GST · MCA · HCES · PLFS · ASUSE · ASI · e-Vahan · PFMS

Indirect-tax records, company filings, new consumption and jobs surveys, and vehicle / public-finance registers feed the estimates.

Sharper methods

Denton benchmarking for quarterly series; double deflation for manufacturing value-added. Less drift between quarters and years.

Coverage

What gets counted better

Services, small firms, and informal activity captured through the newer surveys and administrative data rather than old ratios.

Why statisticians say this is better

The update fixes a 14-year-old photograph of the economy.

  • Post-GST reality. GST returns track formal turnover directly instead of imputing it from old ratios.
  • Company filings (MCA). A wider, cleaner corporate universe replaces thin sample blow-ups.
  • New household maps. The Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) and PLFS refresh spending and jobs weights.
  • Small firms counted. ASUSE plus ASI cover unincorporated and organised manufacturing separately.
  • Cleaner quarters. Denton benchmarking keeps quarterly paths consistent with annual totals; double deflation stops input-price swings from masquerading as manufacturing growth.

The controversy, fairly stated

A rebasing always moves history. Whether it moved it correctly is the argument.

The government’s case

  • 14-year-old base was overdue; global practice is roughly every 5–10 years.
  • Administrative data (GST, MCA, e-Vahan, PFMS) is harder to argue with than survey multipliers.
  • Q1 FY27 printed +7.8% real on the new base — momentum intact.

The sceptics’ case

  • 2023–24 growth cut from 9.2% to 7.2% raises the question: how firm was the old “fastest-growing” narrative?
  • Back-series splicing is thin; HT (3 Sep 2026) cites base 80.00 vs govt 88.27 and Garg’s old 86.05 — Q1 comparisons hinge on which denominator you trust.
  • Nominal levels marked down ~3–4% flatter tax-to-GDP and debt optics; informal-sector capture is still contested.

Bottom line: methods are more modern, but treat any long time-series chart spanning the base change as provisional until MoSPI publishes a full back series.

What to watch next

Four numbers that will show whether the new tape measures straight.

  1. Full back series + deflatorsDo manufacturing and services deflators behave under double deflation, or swing the story quarter to quarter?
  2. Nominal GDP pathA ~3.8% lower denominator lifts debt and deficit ratios — watch the Budget math.
  3. Q2–Q4 FY27 revisionsDenton-benchmarked quarters should revise less. If they don’t, method gains are cosmetic.
  4. Jobs-consumption cross-checkDo PLFS jobs and HCES spending move with the new GVA, or diverge?

Method & data glossary

Denton = quarterly-to-annual benchmarking keeping quarter shapes while hitting annual totals. Double deflation = output and inputs deflated separately for manufacturing GVA. Sources: GSTN turnover, MCA-21 filings, HCES consumption, PLFS employment, ASUSE unincorporated enterprises, ASI organised industry, e-Vahan registrations, PFMS expenditure.
