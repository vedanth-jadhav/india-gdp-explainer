India has a new ruler for its economy. It reads a little shorter.

The GDP base year moved from 2011–12 to 2022–23. Totals step down a few percent, the methods get far more modern, and every old growth comparison now needs a footnote.

Base year 2022–23 FY26 advance estimate +7.6% GDP, +7.7% GVA (MoSPI) Q1 FY27 +7.8% real, reported 3 Sep 2026

“The economy did not shrink; the tape measure was replaced. New tax records, company filings and household surveys stand in for decade-old guesses.”

The 30-second version · read on for the numbers behind it

Every fourteen years or so, India redraws the map of its own economy. The last map was drawn in 2011–12, before GST, before the current company-filings database, before the newest consumption and jobs surveys. The map released on 27 February 2026 starts from 2022–23 instead, and everything measured against it shifts a little. Nominal GDP for 2022–23 comes in 2.9% lower than the old series suggested, and the two following years 3.8% lower, according to government figures carried by The Diplomat. Growth rates move too, in opposite directions: 2023–24 revised from 9.2% to 7.2%, and 2024–25 from 6.5% to 7.1%.

1.What actually changed

One release, three shifts: a new base year, new source data, and stricter quarterly arithmetic.

New base
2022–23 replaces 2011–12. Levels and weights now reflect the post-GST, post-Covid economy rather than the economy of fourteen years ago.
Fresh inputs
GST, MCA, HCES, PLFS, ASUSE, ASI, e-Vahan, PFMS. Indirect-tax records, company filings, new consumption and jobs surveys, and vehicle and public-finance registers feed the estimates.
Sharper methods
Denton benchmarking and double deflation. Quarterly paths are tied to annual totals so quarters stop drifting; manufacturing value-added deflates output and inputs separately, so input-price swings no longer masquerade as growth.
Wider coverage
Services, small firms, informal work. Newer surveys and administrative data replace old ratios and thin sample blow-ups for activity the old series saw poorly.

3.Why statisticians call this better

The update replaces a fourteen-year-old photograph of the economy.

Post-GST reality
GST returns track formal turnover directly instead of imputing it from old ratios.
Company filings
A wider, cleaner MCA corporate universe replaces thin sample blow-ups.
Household maps
The Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) and PLFS refresh spending and jobs weights.
Small firms
ASUSE plus ASI cover unincorporated and organised manufacturing separately.
Cleaner quarters
Denton benchmarking keeps quarterly paths consistent with annual totals; double deflation steadies manufacturing measurement.

4.The controversy, fairly stated

A rebasing always moves history. Whether it moved it correctly is the argument.

The government’s case

  • A fourteen-year-old base was overdue; global practice is roughly every five to ten years.
  • Administrative data (GST, MCA, e-Vahan, PFMS) is harder to argue with than survey multipliers.
  • Q1 FY27 printed +7.8% real on the new base — momentum intact.

The sceptics’ case

  • Cutting 2023–24 growth from 9.2% to 7.2% asks how firm the old “fastest-growing” narrative was.
  • Back-series splicing is thin; HT (3 Sep 2026) cites base 80.00 vs govt 88.27 and Garg’s old 86.05 — Q1 comparisons hinge on which denominator you trust.
  • Nominal levels marked down 3–4% flatter tax-to-GDP and debt optics; informal-sector capture is still contested.
Methods are more modern, but treat any long time-series chart spanning the base change as provisional until MoSPI publishes a full back series.

5.What to watch next

Four numbers that will show whether the new tape measures straight.

  1. Full back series and deflatorsDo manufacturing and services deflators behave under double deflation, or swing the story quarter to quarter?
  2. The nominal GDP pathA roughly 3.8% lower denominator lifts debt and deficit ratios — watch the Budget math.
  3. Q2 to Q4 FY27 revisionsDenton-benchmarked quarters should revise less. If they don’t, the method gains are cosmetic.
  4. Jobs and consumption cross-checkDo PLFS jobs and HCES spending move with the new GVA, or diverge?
